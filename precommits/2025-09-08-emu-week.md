**Fact check behind this file** (Sonnet agent, Sep 2026; abstracts only, since full texts were blocked):
- **Taper practice varies by center, and there's no standard protocol.** A rapid taper to about 50% of the baseline load is common and efficient. Benzodiazepines and barbiturates get slower tapers. Patients with a history of bilateral tonic-clonic seizures (BTCS) or status get gentler steps (about 25%).
- **Length of stay** is usually 3 to 5 days, and presurgical stays average about 7. First events come at about 2 days on average. About a quarter of admissions capture nothing.
- **Insurance:** prior authorization for a set number of days; extra days need concurrent review or a peer-to-peer call.
- **Adverse events** run about 7% of admissions (Sauro 2016 meta-analysis). Clusters are the most common; falls, status, and postictal psychosis are each 1 to 2%.
- **Functional seizures:** "functional seizures" is the current term. About 10 to 13% of patients also have epilepsy, and 20 to 30% at tertiary centers. Roughly 40% accept the diagnosis at the first talk. **Anachronism check (Sep 2026):** the AAN guideline "Management of Functional Seizures" was published online in *Neurology* on **Dec 10, 2025**. In September 2025 it doesn't exist yet, so nobody in-world cites it. Delivery practice in play draws on Hall-Patch (2010) and ordinary good practice.
- **"Three typical seizures" is not a formal target.** The count depends on how well the data agree. At Blue River it's Eric's and the unit's working number (proposed), and Philip can argue with it.

## 1. Kristen Doyle, 38: Phase I presurgical (Eric's referral)

**Who she is:** a middle-school math teacher in Blue Springs, Missouri. Right-handed. Married to **Rob**, a UPS driver. Two kids, 9 and 12. Commercial insurance through the school district.

**History:**
- A prolonged febrile seizure at 18 months (her mother remembers "forty minutes and an ambulance"). Focal epilepsy since age 19.
- **Typical seizures:** a rising feeling in her stomach, then déjà vu ("like I'm about to remember something and I never do"), then she stares, smacks her lips, fumbles with her left hand, and can't talk for a few minutes afterward. Rob has seen her right arm "go stiff and twisted." Three or four a month.
- **Bilateral tonic-clonic:** two in 19 years. The last was in 2021, at school, in front of her class.
- **Failed:** levetiracetam (mood), carbamazepine (hyponatremia), oxcarbazepine (same). **Now on:** lamotrigine 200 mg twice a day and lacosamide 200 mg twice a day.
- **Outside EEG (2023, from a Blue Springs practice):** "independent bitemporal sharp waves, left greater than right." The report estimates about 70/30.
- **3T MRI (July 2025, Blue River):** neuroradiology's report says "subtle left hippocampal volume loss and T2/FLAIR hyperintensity, **possible** left mesial temporal sclerosis." If Philip looks at the images himself, he sees a real but subtle asymmetry: the left hippocampus is a little smaller, a little bright on FLAIR, and has lost some internal architecture. A careful reader calls it left MTS, and a cautious one calls it "possible."
- **Neuropsychology (August 2025, proposed):** mild weakness in verbal memory, everything else average. That points left and it's soft.
- **Not done yet:** fMRI for language, PET, Wada.

**What she wants:** fewer seizures, and above all no more tonic-clonics in front of a classroom. She's read about surgery and laser ablation and she's scared about memory. "I teach. I need my words." She wants a straight answer on the memory risk.

**What Rob wants:** for someone to tell them how long this all takes. He's used a week of vacation for this.

**Hidden and fixed this week:**
- **Seizure 1, Tue Sep 9, about 10:14 a.m.** (draw 0.1124: **clearly left**). A typical seizure: she pushes the button herself and says "it's coming." Stare, lip-smacking, **left-hand fumbling and right arm dystonic posturing** (automatisms on the side of onset, posturing on the opposite side), then two to three minutes of trouble finding words afterward. About 70 seconds. **EEG:** rhythmic 5 to 6 Hz theta building over the left temporal chain (F7, T7, and the left inferior temporal electrodes if they're on), starting about 8 seconds after her first clinical sign, then left temporal slowing afterward. A well-trained reader calls it left temporal onset without much doubt.
- **Seizure 2, Wed Sep 10, about 3:40 a.m.,** out of sleep (draw 0.6897: **nonlateralized**). She's under the covers and the video is poor. **EEG:** diffuse attenuation, then bilateral rhythmic theta, with no side clearly first. An honest read is "not lateralizing."
- **Seizure 3, Thu Sep 11, afternoon** (draw 0.3294: **clearly left**). It starts exactly like seizure 1 on the EEG and on video, **and then it becomes a bilateral tonic-clonic seizure** (draw 0.0215, weighted 15% given her history). About 2 minutes in total. What the nurses do in that moment follows Philip's rescue orders exactly. Afterward she's confused and sore for hours, and Rob is shaken.
- **Seizure 4 happens only if Philip keeps her off medication after seizure 3** (draw 0.9790: **right-first**). Its EEG would start over the right temporal chain before the left. If he restarts her medications after seizure 3, it never happens and nobody ever sees it.
- **If Philip's taper is very slow** (25% a day or less on day one), everything shifts a day later (seizure 1 on Wednesday, seizure 3 and the tonic-clonic on Friday). With a standard or rapid taper, the timeline above holds.
- **Postictal psychosis:** not drawn to occur this week.
- **Whether she truly has one-sided or two-sided epilepsy is NOT decided here.** It gets precommitted before any SEEG, Wada, or surgical result is revealed.

## 2. Amber Kowalski, 25: suspected functional seizures

**Who she is:** a pharmacy technician at a chain pharmacy in Raytown. Lives in south Kansas City with her mother, **Diane**, a school bus driver. On her mother's plan through the school district until she turns 26 in **November** (proposed), so the insurance clock is real to her.

**History:**
- Events for 14 months. Weekly to several times a week, lasting 5 to 20 minutes. Eyes closed, head moving side to side, arms and legs shaking out of sync, waxing and waning. Sometimes she cries afterward. She remembers "pieces."
- **Four ED visits. Intubated once** (April 2025, at an outside hospital, for "status epilepticus"). She hates talking about it.
- **Medicines from the ED and an outside neurologist:** levetiracetam 1,500 mg twice a day and lacosamide 100 mg twice a day. Nothing has changed the events. Two routine EEGs were normal.
- **Stopped driving** 14 months ago on her own. Her mother drives her to work.
- **Life offstage (not volunteered):** her father died of a heart attack two years ago in the house, and she found him. She doesn't connect that to the events and would be angry if someone connected it for her too fast. She comes out with it only if asked, open-endedly, about hard things in the last few years, and only to someone who's already been decent with her.

**What she wants:** a name for this, and to drive and work without being scared. She's afraid of being told she's faking. A nurse at an outside ED once said "she's doing it for attention" loud enough for her to hear.

**What Diane wants:** for somebody to take her daughter seriously. She's protective, she's the one who calls, and she keeps a spiral notebook of events.

**Hidden and fixed:**
- **Does she also have epilepsy?** (draw 0.1558, weighted 15%): **No.** Her events are functional only. No epileptiform discharges show up all week. The draw means Philip isn't secretly wrong if he makes the diagnosis on a well-captured typical event.
- **Captured event: Tue Sep 9, about 2:40 p.m.** A typical event, about 11 minutes. Eyes closed and resisting opening, head side to side, asynchronous shaking, pauses and restarts, crying after, partial recall. **EEG:** a normal posterior rhythm visible through muscle artifact during the event, no evolving rhythm, and no postictal slowing. **Diane confirms on video: "That's it. That's exactly what they look like."**
- **What the nurse does at minute 5 follows Philip's orders from Monday.** If the rescue order says to give lorazepam for any event over 5 minutes and nothing more, the day nurse gives 2 mg IV at minute 5. The event continues to minute 11 anyway, and the EEG shows no correlate. If his order tells the nurse to call the physician or check with the EEG tech before a benzodiazepine for events like this one, the nurse calls him.
- **Her stance toward the diagnosis at baseline** (draw 0.4728): **ambivalent.** She's neither ready to accept it nor ready to reject it. Wednesday's delivery (E03B, scene 4) moves her:
  - **A good delivery** (the event is real and she's not faking; it's common; it has a name; the brain is working in the wrong way, not damaged; what it isn't; a concrete plan to taper the seizure drugs and refer her for therapy with a named next step; room for Diane's questions): she moves to **cautiously accepting.** She'll ask whether she can drive and whether she has to "see a shrink."
  - **An adequate delivery missing one or two of those:** she stays **ambivalent.** She says "okay" and Diane asks for a second opinion.
  - **A poor delivery** (anything that sounds like "it's all in your head," "stress," or "not real," or rushing it): she **rejects it.** Diane says they'll go to KU. There's a portal message complaint within a week.
- **The drugs:** if Philip tapers them off, nothing happens medically, since she doesn't have epilepsy. Whether and how he does it is his call.

## 3. Keith Barlow, 47: reduced medications, no event in five days

**Hidden and fixed:**
